The work investigates to what extent unintentional biases and specific linguistic features, primarily emotional and affective signals, serve as common denominators in the texts used to train Large Language Models (LLMs) and the online texts of various extremist groups, including political and religious extremism as well as manospheres and health-related extremism (such as pro-eating disorder groups). Previous studies have identified radicalised individuals on social media. However, a more prophylactic approach to counter radical communities is to detect individuals prior to getting radicalised. The aim is thus to pinpoint users that are at risk of radicalisation, by examining social media data from multiple sources.

Significant dissimilarities in linguistic and emotional expression patterns were confirmed, allowing for differentiation between ideologies, with classifiers showing a strong capability to reliably distinguish between extremist groups and identify potential school shooters based solely on linguistic characteristics. Big 5 personality traits and emotional patterns also varied between groups, e.g., with “disgust” emerging as a dominant emotion in many extremist discourses, but “joy” being most prominent in the analysed religious extremist content.

As the usage of LLMs quickly increases, so does the significance of the harms they may cause due to bias. The research field of bias in LLMs has seen massive growth, but few attempts have been made to detect or mitigate other biases than gender bias, and most focus has been on English LLMs. This work also shows experimentally that LLMs may cause representational harms towards LGBTQIA+ individuals, but also that queer bias can be altered, laying the foundation for future debiasing approaches. Language-specific patterns are also evident: Norwegian templates yield relatively stable and low gender bias across model families.
