A university teacher can now ask a chatbot to plan next week's lecture, draft comments on forty lab reports, or answer students' questions at midnight. Some teachers do all three. Others will not let it near their course. At NTNU, the guideline on generative AI makes each teacher responsible for deciding how AI may be used in their course, so the line between what the teacher does and what the machine does is drawn one course at a time.

This paper asks where university teachers draw that line, and why. A structured literature review screened 530 records and selected 22 core studies of higher-education teachers and generative AI. Across the teaching cycle, teachers hand over preparation more readily than grading, feedback or answering students. Most studies measure what teachers think of generative AI, not what they actually hand over, and few involve university teachers in designing the tools.

The paper lays the ground for the next stage of the project: a running survey of NTNU teaching staff and co-design workshops where teachers map their current use and where they most need support. The goal is design principles for AI tools that support teachers without taking over their professional judgement. Whatever AI can do in a course, it may be just as useful to ask the teacher one simple question: what would you never hand over?
